Hello Seemab. Today a new Chemistry chapter begins, Chapter eleven, Environmental Chemistry, the air. But first a question, and this one does not come from the book. It comes from our own homes.

Every winter in Pakistan the same news appears: a family left a gas heater running and went to sleep, and in the morning they did not wake up.

Now think about that. If gas leaks, it smells, doesn't it? A smell strong enough to wake anyone at once. So why did these people not wake up? Were they simply sleeping too deeply?

No. The reason is something else, and once you hear it you will never forget it. Hold that thought, we will come back to it.

First, something simple. What is the atmosphere?

The book's words are these: the envelope of gases and water vapour surrounding the planet Earth is called the atmosphere.

So, a covering of gases and water vapour wrapped around the Earth. Picture the Earth wearing a very thin sheet. That sheet is the atmosphere.

And what is in it? Dry air is seventy eight percent nitrogen and twenty one percent oxygen. The rest is a little of the noble gases and carbon dioxide.

Here is a figure the exam often asks for: water vapour is not fixed. In desert air there is very little, and in a tropical rainforest it can be up to four percent. The book says it varies from place to place and from time to time.

Next, what is a pollutant?

Learn the book's definition: anything that is in the air, water or soil which has a harmful effect on some part of the environment is called pollutant.

Notice there are three places in that sentence: air, water and soil. Not only air.

And let me tell you about something printed wrongly in your book. This section is numbered ten point two. That is wrong. This is Unit eleven, so it should be eleven point two. All the subsections after it are numbered correctly, eleven point two point one, eleven point two point two. In the exam, write eleven point two.

Now the six pollutants. Listen to them one at a time, and attach each one to something you can picture.

First, sulphur oxides. Sulphur occurs naturally inside fossil fuels. So when power plants, vehicles, factories and generators burn that fuel, sulphur dioxide comes out. The book gives a rather nice example: silk cloth left in the open air for a week loses its colour. That is this gas at work. And on people? It is absorbed into the respiratory system, and it worsens the suffering of anyone with asthma, bronchitis or emphysema. It is also responsible for acid rain and for haze.

Second, nitrogen oxides. There are two: nitric oxide, NO, and nitrogen dioxide, NO two. Together they are written NOx. The biggest source is fuel burning in vehicles, power plants and generators, and the book says particularly that car engines produce a great deal. The harm: they are highly toxic, they cause acid rain, they cause photochemical smog, and they bring headaches and respiratory problems.

Third, carbon dioxide. It occurs naturally, but human activity has raised it dramatically: burning fossil fuels, cutting down forests, and industrial processes. The harm is climate change and global warming.

Fourth, carbon monoxide. We will come back to this one, because it is the answer to our opening question.

Fifth, methane. Where does it come from? The decomposition of vegetation and waste, from wetlands, from rice fields, from landfills. And from one more place that will make you laugh: the digestive systems of animals. Cows, goats, sheep. The book says plainly that it is released through belching and flatulence. Yes, really. And it increases global warming.

Sixth, ground level ozone, also called smog. This one is the most interesting, so listen closely.

The other five pollutants all come directly out of something: a chimney, an exhaust pipe, a rubbish heap. But ground level ozone comes out of nothing at all. It forms in the air itself, when nitrogen oxides and VOCs, that is volatile organic compounds, react together under sunlight.

That is why the book gives it a special name: a secondary pollutant. Second hand pollution, which was never emitted but was made out of other pollutants. Write that phrase in the exam; the mark is for that phrase.

Now back to the question we left at the start.

Those people do not wake up because the gas killing them is carbon monoxide. And the book says of it plainly: carbon monoxide is a colourless and odourless gas, so its presence cannot be felt.

No colour, no smell. Nothing to notice at all.

And what does it do once inside? It joins with the haemoglobin in the blood and makes carboxyhaemoglobin. And that cannot carry oxygen.

Now think how frightening that is. The breathing continues. Air is going in. The chest rises and falls. But no oxygen is reaching the body, because the carriers that should be carrying it are already full, and full of the wrong thing. The person loses consciousness, and then suffocates.

And when is it formed? When there is not enough oxygen. That is called incomplete combustion.

And now everything connects. In a closed room, a heater burns and the oxygen keeps dropping. And the less oxygen there is, the more carbon monoxide is made. So the more dangerous the room becomes, the more poison it produces.

That is why a room needs air moving through it. Leave the window slightly open. This is not just an exam question, it is a household one, and you can tell your mother too.

Remember this for life: a poison you can smell is the less dangerous kind, because you can run from it.
