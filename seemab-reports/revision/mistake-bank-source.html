📒 Mistake Bank

Seemab's own mistakes, in her own words · 25 September 2026

This is not a report card. It is a list of your own mistakes, pulled out of every paper you have sat, quoted exactly as you wrote them, with the right answer beside each one and one short rule to carry forward.

Each subject has one page. Ten minutes before a paper, read only that subject's page. It is worth more than re-reading your notes, because everything here is something you have actually missed before.

🧭 How to use it
  1. Open only the page for the subject you are about to sit.
  2. Red box first: these are the mistakes that have happened more than once.
  3. Nothing to tick and nothing to write. Just read, and notice.
  4. Green box last: these are habits you have already fixed. That matters just as much as the open ones.

Four things that are the same in every subject

1. Confusable pairs

Your wrong MCQs are nearly always the wrong twin of two similar things: gradient and area, oxidising and reducing, group and period, lymphatic and immune, LCM and HCF, mode and class mark. You know the concept; you pick the wrong half. So learn them as pairs, never alone: on its own, each one sounds right. In the exam, cross out two options first, then compare the remaining pair against each other.

2. Units and names

The cheapest mistake there is. The working is completely right and then g, mol, N, m/s² or the name of the thing never gets written. It has cost marks in Chemistry, Physics, Biology and Maths. Write the answer, then look back at the last line once.

3. The closing sentence

When a question says show that, hence, verify, prove, state or classify, the working alone is not enough. A full sentence has to follow: “Hence P, Q and R are collinear.” This ran for six papers, then you fixed it, and it still comes back now and then.

4. Copying a worked example’s direction

When a question is the reverse of the book's example, the book's number comes to mind and gets written down. Chemistry exam-25 Q.7 gave ΔH as −11 when the answer was +11; exam-23 had the ionisation equations the wrong way round. Check which side is the reactant first, then put the values in.

One more small thing

Sometimes the right answer is in your head and a different one reaches the paper. Physics exam-15 Q3: your reason proved B and you wrote A. Maths exam-18 Q7: your working reached C and you wrote D. After writing the reason, look back at the options once.
