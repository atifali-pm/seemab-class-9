SECTION A — Multiple Choice Questions Answer Key (12 × 1 = 12 Marks)
Q1. A chemical reaction that proceeds with the evolution of heat is called:   ✓ B — Exothermic reaction 1 mark
An exothermic reaction releases heat to the surroundings; the system loses energy so ΔH is negative. Endothermic reactions absorb heat (positive ΔH). Thermochemical reaction is the broader category, not the specific type that releases heat.
Q2. If the ΔH value of a reaction is negative, the reaction is:   ✓ B — Exothermic 1 mark
By the sign convention in Section 8.3: ΔH is negative for exothermic reactions (heat flows out of the system) and positive for endothermic reactions. A common student error is to confuse the sign, believing negative ΔH means the reaction absorbs energy.
Q3. C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −393.5 kJ. This reaction is:   ✓ B — Exothermic 1 mark
The negative sign on ΔH° = −393.5 kJ confirms energy is released to the surroundings. This is also the textbook example given in Section 8.2.1 for combustion of carbon, a classic exothermic reaction.
Q4. Bond breaking is always:   ✓ B — Endothermic 1 mark
Section 8.4 states: "Breaking bonds always requires energy" (endothermic), while forming bonds always releases energy (exothermic). Students often get this backwards, thinking breaking bonds releases energy.
Q5. The enthalpy change of a reaction equals:   ✓ C — Sum of BDE of reactants minus sum of BFE of products 1 mark
The textbook formula (Section 8.4): ΔH° = Sum of bond dissociation energies of reactants − Sum of bond formation energies of products. Option D reverses the subtraction, a common sign error that students make.
Q6. Activation energy must be _______ the average kinetic energy:   ✓ C — greater than 1 mark
Section 8.5 (exercise Q(v)) states activation energy must be greater than the average kinetic energy of the reacting molecules for an effective collision to occur. If particles do not have enough energy, collisions are ineffective and no reaction takes place.
Q7. A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction by:   ✓ C — lowering activation energy 1 mark
Section 8.6: a catalyst provides a new reaction mechanism with lower activation energy. It does not change the overall enthalpy (ΔH remains the same). Students often wrongly choose "increasing enthalpy," confusing the energy diagram peak with the overall energy change.
Q8. A balanced chemical equation that also shows the heat change is called:   ✓ B — Thermochemical equation 1 mark
Section 8.2 definition: "A balanced chemical equation which also shows heat change of a chemical reaction is called thermochemical equation." The branch of chemistry studying these is thermochemistry.
Q9. Which product is NOT released in aerobic respiration?   ✓ C — Lactic acid 1 mark
Lactic acid is the product of anaerobic respiration (Glucose → Lactic acid + Energy). Aerobic respiration produces carbon dioxide, water, and energy. Lactic acid builds up in muscles during intense exercise when oxygen supply is insufficient.
Q10. Which reaction has a positive enthalpy change?   ✓ C — N2(g) + O2(g) → 2NO(g), ΔH° = +180.5 kJ 1 mark
A positive ΔH indicates an endothermic reaction. Options A, B, and D all have negative ΔH values (exothermic). The formation of NO from N2 and O2 absorbs energy (+180.5 kJ), making it endothermic, as listed in Section 8.2.2 of the textbook.
Q11. Lipids act as reserve energy stores because:   ✓ B — they can store very large amounts of energy 1 mark
Section 8.7 states: "Lipids can store very large amounts of energy in our body." When glucose oxidation is insufficient during intense exercise, the body oxidizes lipids for energy. Lipids are not catalysts or water-soluble compounds.
Q12. In living organisms, the catalysts that catalyze chemical reactions are called:   ✓ C — Enzymes 1 mark
Section 8.6 states: "In the bodies of living organisms enzymes catalyze chemical reactions." Enzymes are biological catalysts that lower activation energy for biochemical reactions. Vitamins and hormones serve different roles; lipids are energy stores.

2(i) — Chemical energetics and thermochemical reactions 3 marks
Chemical energetics: The study of energy changes in chemical reactions is called chemical energetics. 1 mark
Thermochemical reaction: A chemical reaction which proceeds with the evolution or absorption of heat is called a thermochemical reaction. A balanced chemical equation that also shows the heat change is called a thermochemical equation. 1 mark
Example: C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g)    ΔH° = −393.5 kJ 1 mark
OR
System: The part of the universe that we want to focus our attention on is called the system. In chemistry, the system is usually the substance that changes physically or chemically. 1 mark
Surroundings: The rest of the universe (everything around the system) is called the environment or surroundings. 1 mark
Example: When studying the reaction of limestone and hydrochloric acid in a test tube, the limestone and HCl solution form the system. The test tube, heat source, and everything around it form the surroundings. 1 mark
2(ii) — Exothermic and endothermic reactions with examples 3 marks
Exothermic reaction: A chemical reaction that proceeds with the evolution of heat is called an exothermic reaction. The chemical system transfers energy to the surroundings; ΔH is negative. 1 mark
Examples: 2 marks
  • C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g)    ΔH° = −393.5 kJ
  • 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(g)    ΔH° = −571.6 kJ
OR
Endothermic reaction: A chemical reaction that proceeds with the absorption of heat is called an endothermic reaction. Heat is transferred from surroundings to the system; ΔH is positive. 1 mark
Examples: 2 marks
  • H2(g) + I2(g) → 2HI(g)    ΔH° = +53.8 kJ
  • N2(g) + O2(g) → 2NO(g)    ΔH° = +180.5 kJ
2(iii) — Sign convention for enthalpy change 3 marks
The amount of heat or thermal energy evolved or absorbed in a chemical reaction is called enthalpy of reaction. 1 mark
Sign convention:
  • ΔH is negative for exothermic reactions: the system releases energy to the surroundings, so the system loses heat (energy decreases). 1 mark
  • ΔH is positive for endothermic reactions: the system absorbs energy from the surroundings, so the system gains heat (energy increases). 1 mark
OR
Standard enthalpy change (ΔH°): Enthalpy of reaction measured at 25°C (298 K) and one atmospheric pressure is known as standard enthalpy change. 1 mark
It is denoted by the symbol ΔH°. 1 mark
Example: C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −393.5 kJ 1 mark
2(iv) — Classify processes as exothermic or endothermic 3 marks
(a) Freezing of water: Exothermic (heat is released when liquid water converts to solid ice). 1 mark
(b) Combustion of methane: Exothermic (burning releases large amounts of heat energy). 1 mark
(c) Decomposition of limestone (CaCO3 → CaO + CO2): Endothermic (heat must be supplied to break down limestone). 1 mark
OR
(a) Sublimation of dry ice: Endothermic (solid CO2 absorbs heat from surroundings to convert directly to gas). 1 mark
(b) H2O(g) → H2O(l): Exothermic (condensation releases heat, as steam gives out energy when it becomes liquid). 1 mark
(c) Burning of fuels: Exothermic (combustion releases heat and light energy to the surroundings). 1 mark
2(v) — Bond dissociation energy and bond formation energy 3 marks
Bond dissociation energy (BDE): The amount of energy required to break one mole of a particular bond to form neutral atoms is called the bond dissociation energy. Bond breaking always absorbs energy (endothermic). 1 mark
Bond formation energy (BFE): The amount of energy released when neutral atoms form one mole of a bond is called the bond formation energy. Bond forming always releases energy (exothermic). 1 mark
Relationship: ΔH° = Sum of BDE of reactants − Sum of BFE of products. The difference between these two determines whether the overall reaction absorbs or releases energy. 1 mark
OR
Bond breaking absorbs energy because energy must be put in to overcome the attraction between atoms. This is an endothermic process. 1 mark
Bond forming releases energy because when atoms form a bond, they move to a lower energy state, releasing the energy difference. This is an exothermic process. 1 mark
Overall: If the energy absorbed in breaking bonds (reactants) is less than the energy released in forming bonds (products), the net reaction is exothermic (ΔH < 0). If breaking bonds requires more energy than is released in forming bonds, the reaction is endothermic (ΔH > 0). 1 mark
2(vi) — ΔH formula and bond energy calculation 3 marks
Formula: ΔH° = Sum of bond dissociation energies of reactants − Sum of bond formation energies of products 1 mark
(a) If sum of BDE of reactants > sum of BFE of products: ΔH is positive (endothermic reaction, net energy absorbed). 1 mark
(b) If sum of BDE of reactants < sum of BFE of products: ΔH is negative (exothermic reaction, net energy released). 1 mark
OR
Calculation (H2 + I2 → 2HI): 3 marks
Equation: H-H + I-I --> 2 H-I H2(g) + I2(g) --> 2HI(g) DeltaH = [BDE(H-H) + BDE(I-I)] - [2 x BFE(H-I)] = [436 + 151] - [2 x 299] = 587 - 598 = -11 kJ/mol Result: DeltaH = -11 kJ/mol (Exothermic, negative value)
Bonds broken (reactants): H−H (436) + I−I (151) = 587 kJ. Bonds formed (products): 2 × H−I (299) = 598 kJ. Since 598 > 587, more energy is released than absorbed, so ΔH is negative and the reaction is exothermic.
2(vii) — Activation energy and collision theory 3 marks
Activation energy: The minimum amount of energy that, in addition to the average kinetic energy, particles must have in effective collisions is called the activation energy. 1 mark
Effective collision: A collision in which reacting particles have sufficient energy (at least equal to activation energy) AND correct orientation, causing bonds to break and new bonds to form. 1 mark
Ineffective collision: A collision in which particles do not have enough energy or do not have the correct orientation, so the particles simply bounce off without reacting. 1 mark
OR
Two conditions for an effective collision: (1) The colliding particles must have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy. (2) The particles must collide with the correct orientation so that the atoms needing to form new bonds actually collide with each other. 2 marks
Higher activation energy and slower rate: If activation energy is higher, fewer particles in a sample will have enough energy to react at any given moment, so fewer effective collisions occur per second, resulting in a slower reaction rate. 1 mark
2(viii) — Catalyst and catalysis 3 marks
Catalyst: A substance that accelerates a chemical reaction but remains chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction is called a catalyst. 1 mark
Catalysis: The phenomenon in which the rate of a chemical reaction is increased by a catalyst is called catalysis. 1 mark
Mechanism: The catalyst provides a new reaction pathway with lower activation energy. More particles can now reach the activation energy threshold, so more effective collisions occur per second. Importantly, the catalyst does not affect the overall thermodynamics or enthalpy of the reaction; the energy difference between reactants and products (i.e., ΔH) stays the same. 1 mark
OR
Enzyme: In the bodies of living organisms, the biological catalysts that catalyze chemical reactions are called enzymes. 1 mark
Difference from industrial catalysts: Enzymes are highly specific, meaning each enzyme typically catalyzes only one type of reaction or works on only one type of substrate molecule. Industrial chemical catalysts (e.g., iron in the Haber process) are generally less specific and can catalyze a range of reactions under appropriate conditions. 2 marks
2(ix) — Respiration as an exothermic process 3 marks
Respiration: Respiration is a biochemical process in which energy is released from food in a biological system. During this process, glucose is oxidized in the body of living organisms and energy is released. 1 mark
Why exothermic: In respiration, glucose is oxidized (bonds broken and new bonds formed), and the energy released in forming products (CO2, H2O) is greater than the energy absorbed in breaking the bonds of reactants (glucose, O2). The net result is a release of energy, so ΔH is negative, making it exothermic. 2 marks
OR
Aerobic respiration word equation:
    Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy 1 mark
Reactants: Glucose (food / stored chemical energy) and Oxygen (from breathing). 1 mark
Products: Carbon dioxide (exhaled), Water (released), and Energy (used for all body functions). The energy comes from the breaking of chemical bonds in glucose, with more energy released in forming CO2 and H2O bonds than was absorbed. 1 mark
2(x) — Aerobic vs anaerobic respiration 3 marks
Aerobic respiration: Respiration that requires oxygen to break down glucose to release energy. 1 mark
    Word equation: Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy

Anaerobic respiration: Respiration that does not require oxygen to break down glucose to release energy. 1 mark
    Word equation: Glucose → Lactic acid + Energy 1 mark
OR
Aerobic releases more energy than anaerobic respiration because complete oxidation of glucose in the presence of oxygen releases more energy than the partial breakdown that occurs without oxygen. 1 mark
Role of lipids during intense exercise: During intense exercise, the body's demand for energy is very high and the oxidation of glucose alone may not be sufficient. At this point, lipids are oxidized for energy. Lipids act as reserve energy stores because they can store very large amounts of energy in the body, providing a secondary fuel source when glucose is depleted. 2 marks
2(xi) — Reaction pathway diagram features 3 marks
For an exothermic reaction pathway diagram:
  • (a) Reactants level: starting energy level on the left, at a higher position than products. 1 mark
  • (b) Products level: ending energy level on the right, at a lower position than reactants (energy released). 1 mark
  • (c) Activation energy: the energy from the reactants level up to the peak (transition state) at the top of the curve. half
  • (d) Enthalpy change (ΔH): the difference in energy between reactants and products, shown as a downward arrow on the right side of the curve; ΔH is negative. half
OR
For an endothermic reaction pathway diagram:
  • (a) Reactants level: starting energy level on the left, at a lower position than products.
  • (b) Products level: ending energy level on the right, higher than reactants (energy absorbed).
  • (c) Activation energy: energy from reactants level up to the transition state peak.
  • (d) Enthalpy change (ΔH): difference in energy between products and reactants, shown as an upward arrow; ΔH is positive because products have more energy than reactants.

3(iii) — Activation energy, collision theory, and reaction pathway diagram 5 marks
(a) Activation energy and collision theory [3 marks]
Activation energy: The minimum amount of energy that, in addition to the average kinetic energy, particles must have in effective collisions is called the activation energy. No reaction occurs if the energy of the reacting particles is lower than the activation energy. 1 mark
Effective collision: A collision is effective only when (i) particles possess energy at least equal to the activation energy AND (ii) the particles have correct orientation so that the atoms needing to form new bonds collide with each other directly. This produces an unstable activated complex at the transition state, which then breaks down to give products. 1 mark
Ineffective collision: If particles lack sufficient energy or have wrong orientation, they simply bounce off one another without reacting. The higher the activation energy, the fewer particles have enough energy and the slower the reaction rate. 1 mark

(a) Catalyst definition and mechanism [3 marks]
Catalyst: A substance that accelerates a chemical reaction but remains chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction. The phenomenon is called catalysis. 1 mark
How it increases rate without changing ΔH: The catalyst provides a new reaction pathway with lower activation energy. Because the energy barrier is lower, more molecules in the sample have sufficient energy to react, so more effective collisions occur per unit time and the rate increases. However, the reactants and products remain the same and their energy levels are unchanged, so the overall enthalpy change (ΔH) is not affected. 2 marks

(a) Respiration, exothermic nature, aerobic vs anaerobic [3 marks]
Respiration: Respiration is a biochemical process in which energy is released from food (glucose) in a biological system. Glucose is oxidized and energy is released; therefore, respiration is an exothermic process (ΔH is negative). 1 mark

(b) Enzymes as biological catalysts and activation energy [2 marks]
Enzymes are biological molecules (proteins) that act as catalysts in living organisms. Like any catalyst, enzymes lower the activation energy of biochemical reactions, allowing them to proceed rapidly at body temperature (37°C) without requiring the high temperatures that industrial reactions often need. Each enzyme is highly specific, catalyzing only one reaction or type of reaction. Without enzymes, the activation energy for most biochemical reactions would be too high for them to occur at the speeds necessary to sustain life. 2 marks
