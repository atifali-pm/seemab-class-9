SECTION A — Multiple Choice Questions Answer Key (12 × 1 = 12 Marks)
Q1. Organic compounds are defined as hydrocarbons and their:   ✓ B — derivatives 1 mark
The textbook (section 13.1) states: "Organic compounds are also defined as the hydrocarbons and their derivatives." Allotropes and isotopes relate to elements, not compound classes. Isomers are a structural phenomenon, not the defining scope of organic chemistry.
Q2. Adjacent members of a homologous series differ by:   ✓ B — −CH2− 1 mark
Section 13.2 defines a homologous series as one in which "any two adjacent molecules differ by −CH2− group." The methyl group (−CH3) has 3 H atoms; −C2H4− is double the unit; −OH is a functional group, not the repeating structural difference.
Q3. General formula of alkanes:   ✓ B — CnH2n+2 1 mark
Table 13.1 lists alkane general formula as CnH2n+2. Option A (CnH2n) is for alkenes. Option C (CnH2n−2) is for alkynes. Option D (CnH2n+1OH) is for alcohols. A common error is confusing alkane and alkene formulae.
Q4. Same molecular formula C4H10, different structures:   ✓ B — isomerism 1 mark
Section 13.5: "The compounds that have the same molecular formula but different arrangement of atoms in their molecules are called structural isomers. This phenomenon is called isomerism." Allotropy is for elements; homology means successive members of the same series; catenation is carbon's chain-forming ability.
Q5. CH3−CH=CH2 and CH2=CH−CH3 (same formula, double bond at same position):   ✓ B — positional isomerism 1 mark
These two are in fact the same compound written in reverse (propene). The classic textbook example of positional isomers from section 13.5 is 1-butene (CH3−CH2−CH=CH2) and 2-butene (CH3−CH=CH−CH3): same molecular formula C4H8, double bond at different positions. This type of structural isomerism where the functional group shifts position is called positional isomerism. Chain isomerism involves a different carbon skeleton.
Q6. −OH attached to an alkyl chain:   ✓ C — alcohol 1 mark
Section 13.7.2: "Alcohols are characterized by the presence of the hydroxyl group −OH attached to a hydrocarbon chain." If −OH is attached to a benzene ring instead, the compound is a phenol (section 13.7.3). Ethers have oxygen between two alkyl groups; aldehydes have −CHO.
Q7. Carbonyl carbon bonded to one H (−CHO) identifies:   ✓ D — aldehyde 1 mark
Section 13.7.5: "An aldehyde has at least one hydrogen atom attached to the carbonyl carbon atom." The condensed form is written as −CHO. A ketone has two alkyl groups on the carbonyl carbon (no H). An ester has −C(=O)−O−; a carboxylic acid has −COOH. Students frequently confuse aldehyde (−CHO) with ketone (−CO−).
Q8. Carbonyl carbon in a ketone is bonded to:   ✓ C — two alkyl groups 1 mark
Section 13.7.5: "A ketone has two hydrocarbon groups (alkyl) bonded to the carbonyl carbon atom." This distinguishes ketones from aldehydes (one H on carbonyl C). Propanone (acetone, CH3COCH3) is the textbook example. Option A describes an aldehyde; option B describes a carboxylic acid functional arrangement.
Q9. CH3−O−CH3 belongs to:   ✓ C — ether 1 mark
Section 13.7.4: "Organic compounds that have two alkyl groups attached to the same oxygen atom are called ethers. These compounds have C−O−C linkage." CH3−O−CH3 is named dimethyl ether in the textbook. A common error is confusing this C−O−C pattern with an alcohol (R−OH) or ester (R−C(=O)−O−R').
Q10. Functional group of carboxylic acids:   ✓ C — −COOH 1 mark
Section 13.7.6: "The functional group of organic acid is called the carboxyl group, written as −COOH." This combines a carbonyl (−C=O) with a hydroxyl (−OH) on the same carbon. Option A (−CHO) is the aldehyde group. Option B (−CO−) is the ketone carbonyl. Option D (−COO−) is part of the ester linkage but lacks the H.
Q11. Which compound is an ester?   ✓ C — CH3COOCH3 1 mark
Section 13.7.7: esters have general formula R−C(=O)−O−R'. CH3COOCH3 is methyl ethanoate (methyl acetate), given as the first textbook example. Option A (CH3COOH) is ethanoic acid (carboxylic acid). Option B (CH3COCH3) is propanone (ketone). Option D (CH3CHO) is ethanal (aldehyde). Confusing ester (−COO−) with ether (−O−) or carboxylic acid (−COOH) is a very common error.
Q12. Carbon's ability to bond with other carbons to form chains:   ✓ B — catenation 1 mark
Section 13.5: "This self-linking ability of carbon is called catenation. There appears to be almost no limit to the number of different structures that carbon can form." Isomerism refers to same-formula different-structure compounds; homology describes the series pattern; saturation refers to bond type, not chain formation.
SECTION B — Short Questions Answer Key (11 × 3 = 33 Marks)
2(i) Organic chemistry definition + exclusions 3 marks
Definition 1 mark: The study of carbon-containing compounds and their properties is called organic chemistry.

Why CO2, CO, and carbonates are excluded 2 marks: Although these compounds contain carbon, they are classified as inorganic substances because they have completely different properties from organic compounds. They lack the carbon-hydrogen (C−H) backbone that characterises organic molecules. The textbook (p. 174) specifically lists CO2, CO, carbonates and carbides as exceptions.
OR
Organic compounds defined 1 mark: Carbon-containing compounds (hydrocarbons and their derivatives) whose essential constituent is carbon in combination with other elements.

Four common elements besides carbon 2 marks: Hydrogen (H), Oxygen (O), Nitrogen (N), Sulphur (S) (also Phosphorus and halogens are acceptable, as stated in section 13.1).

Four general characteristics 2 marks (1 mark for any two, 2 marks for all four):
  • Family members have the same functional group.
  • Family members have the same general formula.
  • Adjacent members differ by a −CH2− unit.
  • Family members display a trend in physical properties.
  • Family members possess similar chemical properties.

2(iv) Saturated vs unsaturated hydrocarbons 3 marks
Saturated 1 mark: Hydrocarbons whose carbon-carbon bonds are all single bonds are called saturated. Examples: methane (CH4), ethane (CH3CH3).

Unsaturated 1 mark: Hydrocarbons containing one or more carbon-carbon multiple bonds (double or triple) are called unsaturated. Examples: ethene (CH2=CH2), ethyne (HC≡CH).

Unsaturated types 1 mark: Alkenes have at least one C=C double bond (general formula CnH2n); alkynes have at least one C≡C triple bond (general formula CnH2n−2).
OR
Identification with reasons 3 marks (1 mark for any two correct, max 3):
  • (a) CH3−CH2−CH3 — saturated: all C−C single bonds.
  • (b) CH3−C≡CH — unsaturated: contains a C≡C triple bond.
  • (c) CH3−CH=CH2 — unsaturated: contains a C=C double bond.
  • (d) CH2=CH−CH=CH2 — unsaturated: contains two C=C double bonds.
2(v) Structural isomers of C4H10 3 marks
Definition 1 mark: Compounds that have the same molecular formula but different arrangement of atoms in their molecules are called structural isomers.

Two isomers of C4H10 2 marks:

Both have molecular formula C4H8 but differ in the position of the double bond. (Ref: section 13.5, textbook example.)
2(vi) Open-chain vs closed-chain compounds 3 marks
Open-chain (acyclic) 1 mark: Compounds that contain an open chain of carbon atoms — carbon atoms linked in a linear (straight or branched) pattern. Examples: propane (CH3CH2CH3), iso-butane.

Closed-chain (cyclic) 1 mark: Organic compounds which contain rings of atoms are called cyclic compounds. Examples: cyclopropane, benzene.

Subcategories of open-chain 1 mark: Open-chain compounds may be straight-chain (all carbons in one continuous chain) or branched-chain (carbon side-groups attached to the main chain).
OR
Homocyclic vs heterocyclic 3 marks:
  • Homocyclic (carbocyclic): rings contain only carbon atoms. Example: benzene (C6H6), cyclopropane. 1 mark
  • Heterocyclic: rings contain one or more atoms other than carbon (e.g. N, O, S). Examples from textbook: pyridine (N in ring), furan (O in ring), thiophene (S in ring). 2 marks
2(vii) Functional group definition and importance 3 marks
Definition 1 mark: "An atom or group of atoms that gives a family of organic compounds its characteristic chemical and physical properties is called a functional group." (Section 13.7)

Three reasons for importance 2 marks (1 mark for any two):
  1. Functional groups serve as the basis for naming organic compounds.
  2. They are used to classify organic compounds into different classes (all compounds with the same functional group belong to the same class).
  3. A functional group is the site of chemical reactivity in a molecule — it determines what reactions the compound undergoes.
OR
Functional group determines properties 3 marks: The properties of organic compounds are predominantly determined by the functional group present. For example, ethanol (CH3CH2OH) and dimethyl ether (CH3−O−CH3) both have molecular formula C2H6O, but ethanol has the −OH (hydroxyl) group so it acts as an alcohol (miscible with water, higher boiling point), while dimethyl ether has the C−O−C linkage so it is an ether (used as anaesthetic, more volatile). Same formula, completely different properties — caused by different functional groups.

2(x) Carboxylic acids + daily-life examples 3 marks
Definition 1 mark: The functional group of organic acids is called the carboxyl group (−COOH). The general formula is R−COOH.

Methanoic acid (HCOOH) 1 mark: Also called formic acid; structural formula H−C(=O)−OH. Found in ant stings and stinging nettles.

Ethanoic acid (CH3COOH) 1 mark: Also called acetic acid; structural formula CH3−C(=O)−OH. Present in vinegar (the textbook states it is used to flavour food and make polyvinyl acetate).
OR
Esters definition 1 mark: Compounds having general formula R−C(=O)−O−R' are called esters. R and R' are alkyl groups which may be the same or different.

Two named examples 2 marks:
  • Methyl ethanoate (methyl acetate): CH3−C(=O)−OCH3
  • Ethyl ethanoate (ethyl acetate): CH3−C(=O)−OCH2CH3
2(xi) Amines and amides 3 marks
Amines 1.5 marks: Functional group −NH2; general formula R−NH2. Example: methylamine (CH3−NH2).

Amides 1.5 marks: Functional group −C(=O)−NH2; general formula R−C(=O)−NH2. Example: ethanamide (CH3−C(=O)−NH2).
OR
Catenation 1 mark: Carbon's self-linking ability to bond with other carbon atoms and form long chains, branched chains and rings is called catenation.

Contribution to diversity 1 mark: Because carbon can form chains of thousands of atoms with no apparent limit, the number of possible structures is enormous. No other element can compete with carbon in this regard (silicon can only form short chains).

Two other factors 1 mark: (1) Isomerism — same molecular formula can give many different structural arrangements; (2) Functional groups — replacing H atoms with different functional groups creates new classes of compounds with distinct properties.

Definition: A series of related compounds in which adjacent molecules differ by a −CH2− unit is called a homologous series.
Five general characteristics (from section 13.2.1):
  1. Family members have the same functional group.
  2. They have the same general formula.

(a) Structural isomerism and catenation 3 marks
Catenation 1 mark: Carbon's ability to bond with other carbon atoms to form chains, branched chains and rings. No other element can form chains as long as carbon — silicon forms only short chains. This generates an enormous variety of structures.
Structural isomerism 1 mark: Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements are structural isomers. A single molecular formula can produce many different compounds, each with different physical and chemical properties.
Combined effect 1 mark: Together, catenation (many chain lengths and shapes possible) and isomerism (multiple structures per formula) account for millions of organic compounds from relatively few elements.

(b) Structural isomers of C4H10 2 marks:
Isomer 1 — n-Butane (straight chain): CH3-CH2-CH2-CH3 Isomer 2 — iso-Butane (branched chain): CH3 | CH3-CH-CH3
Both have molecular formula C4H10; n-butane has a straight carbon chain while iso-butane has a methyl branch on the second carbon.
