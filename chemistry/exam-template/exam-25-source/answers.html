SECTION A: ANSWER KEY (10 × 1 = 10 MARKS)
1. Cannot be classified as an Arrhenius acid: (c) CO2 1
An Arrhenius acid must ionize in water to give H+. CO2 has no hydrogen, so it cannot, even though it is acidic. This is one of the limitations of the Arrhenius theory.
Source: Ch 10, Review Question 1(i), book p147, and section 10.1.2, p142.
2. A Bronsted-Lowry base: (a) NH3 1
NH3 accepts a proton to become NH4+. HCl and CH3COOH are proton donors (acids), and H3O+ is itself an acid.
Source: Ch 10, Review Question 1(ii), book p147.
3. Salt formed when Mg(OH)2 neutralizes HCl: (c) MgCl2 1
Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl → MgCl2 + 2H2O. The salt takes the metal from the base and the Cl from hydrochloric acid.
Source: Ch 10, Review Question 1(iii), book p147; neutralization, section 10.3.
4. Ammonia is a Bronsted-Lowry base because it: (d) accepts a proton 1
Ammonia contains no OH group, which is exactly why the Arrhenius theory could not explain it. Bronsted-Lowry does: it accepts a proton and becomes NH4+.
Source: Ch 10, Review Question 1(iv), book p147, and section 10.1.2, p142.
5. A weak base: (b) Al(OH)3 1
The book: “A base that ionizes to a little extent is called a weak base... Al(OH)3, NH3 etc are weak bases.” NaOH, KOH and Ca(OH)2 are its examples of strong bases.
Source: Ch 10, section 10.2.2, book p144.
6. A property acids and bases share: (d) their aqueous solutions conduct electricity 1
In Table 10.1 the two shared rows are corrosive to skin and aqueous solutions conduct electricity. Only the second is offered here.
Source: Ch 10, Table 10.1, book p141.
7. A strong base: (a) completely dissociates in aqueous solution 1
Strength means how completely it ionizes, not how well it dissolves. A weak base dissociates only partially.
Source: Ch 10, Key Points and section 10.2.2, book p144 and p147.
8. [REVISION, Ch 8] ΔH° = +131.4 kJ means the reaction is: (b) endothermic 1
ΔH is positive for endothermic reactions (heat is absorbed) and negative for exothermic ones. The book lists this exact reaction under endothermic reactions.
Source: Ch 8, sections 8.2.2 and 8.3, book p129.
9. [REVISION, Ch 6] Mass of 0.75 mol of CO2: (b) 33 g 1
Molar mass = 12 + 2(16) = 44 g/mol. Mass = 0.75 × 44 = 33 g.
10. [REVISION, Ch 6] Moles in 54 g of water: (a) 3 mol 1
Molar mass = 2(1) + 16 = 18 g/mol. Moles = 54 ÷ 18 = 3 mol. 0.33 mol is the trap: that comes from dividing 18 by 54.

2. Arrhenius acids, strong acid, weak base. 4
(i) An Arrhenius acid is a substance that ionizes in water to produce H+ ions. 1 Any two examples: HCl, HNO3, H2SO4. 1
(ii) HCl ionizes completely (100%) in water; all of its molecules ionize. 1
(iii) Ammonia ionizes only to a little extent in water, so it produces fewer OH− ions. 1
Source: Ch 10, sections 10.1.1 (p141), 10.2.1 and 10.2.2 (p144); Review Question 2(ii), (iii), (v), p148.
3. Limitations of the Arrhenius theory; Bronsted-Lowry explanation. 4
(i) Any two, 1 mark each:
  • It applies only to aqueous solutions.
  • It cannot explain why CO2 and SO2 are acidic: they contain no H.
  • It cannot explain why NH3 is a base: it contains no OH.
(ii) HNO3 donates a proton (H+), so it is the acid. 1 NH3 accepts that proton and becomes NH4+, so it is the base. 1
Source: Ch 10, section 10.1.2, book p142; Review Question 5(ii), p148.
4. Bronsted-Lowry pair; ionization of two bases. 4
(i) HNO3 is the acid: it donates a proton and becomes NO3−. 1 H2O is the base: it accepts the proton and becomes H3O+. 1 H3O+ is a product; naming it as the acid earns 0 for that part.
(ii)(a)   KOH → K+ + OH−   (single arrow) ½
     (b)   NH4OH ⇌ NH4+ + OH−   (reversible arrow) ½
Correct arrows (single for KOH, reversible for NH4OH) ½. NH4OH is the weak base ½.
Source: Ch 10, section 10.1.1 (p142, which prints both equations with these arrows) and 10.2.2 (p144); Review Question 5(i), p148.

7. [REVISION, Ch 8] ΔH for 2HI → H2 + I2. 4
ΔH = Sum of bond dissociation energies of reactants − Sum of bond formation energies of products 1
ΔH = [2 × 299] − [436 + 151] = 598 − 587 1
ΔH = +11 kJ/mol 1 (sign and unit required; a missing unit loses ½)
ΔH is positive, so the reaction is endothermic: more energy is absorbed breaking the H–I bonds than is released forming H–H and I–I. 1
Source: Ch 8, section 8.4, book p129–130. The data are the book’s Example 8.1, which does the forward reaction and gets −11 kJ/mol. This is the reverse on purpose: reversing the reaction reverses the sign of ΔH. A student who copies −11 from the example loses the answer and conclusion marks.

ReactionBronsted-Lowry acidBronsted-Lowry base

9. [REVISION, Ch 6] Zinc and hydrochloric acid, 6.5 g of Zn. 8
(i) moles of Zn = mass ÷ molar mass = 6.5 ÷ 65 = 0.1 mol 1
(ii) From the equation, 1 mol Zn needs 2 mol HCl, so moles of HCl = 2 × 0.1 = 0.2 mol 1; molar mass of HCl = 1 + 35.5 = 36.5 g/mol, so mass = 0.2 × 36.5 = 7.3 g 1
(iii) 1 mol Zn gives 1 mol H2, so moles of H2 = 0.1 mol 1; mass = 0.1 × 2 = 0.2 g 1
(iv) molecules of H2 = 0.1 × 6.02 × 1023 = 6.02 × 1022 molecules 1
(v) molar mass of ZnCl2 = 65 + 2(35.5) = 136 g/mol; mass = 0.1 × 136 = 13.6 g 1
(vi) “6.5 g of zinc produces 0.2 g of hydrogen gas and 13.6 g of zinc chloride.” 1
Marking note: the 2 : 1 ratio in (ii) is the step to watch; using 0.1 mol of HCl loses that mark but carries forward correctly. Each answer needs its unit (½ off if missing). Reaction from Ch 10, section 10.3 (p145); mole calculations from Ch 6.
