SECTION A — ANSWER KEY (10 × 1 = 10 MARKS)
1. In NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4+ + OH−, water is behaving as a — (c) Bronsted acid 1
Water donates a proton to the ammonia molecule and is left as OH−. A Bronsted-Lowry acid is a proton donor, so in this particular reaction water is the acid and NH3 is the base. Water can act as an acid in one reaction and a base in another, which is why it is called amphoteric.
Source: Ch 10, section 10.1.2, book p143 (“H2O is converted to OH− by donating a proton, so H2O is an acid”). SLO: define Bronsted-Lowry acids as proton donors and bases as proton acceptors.
2. A base but not an alkali — (b) Cu(OH)2 1
An alkali is a base that dissolves in water. Copper hydroxide is a hydroxide of a metal, so it is a base, but it is insoluble in water, so it is not an alkali. NaOH, KOH and Ca(OH)2 all dissolve, so all three are alkalis.
Source: Ch 10, “Alkalis”, book p145. SLO: recognize that bases are oxides or hydroxides of metals and that alkalis are water-soluble bases.
3. A strong acid — (a) It dissociates completely into ions in aqueous solution 1
Strength is about how completely the acid ionizes, not about how much of it dissolves and not about how many hydrogen atoms the formula shows. HCl, HNO3 and H2SO4 ionize 100% in water, so they are strong. Option (d) is a property of a base, not an acid.
Source: Ch 10, section 10.2.1, book p144. SLO: define a strong acid as one that completely dissociates in aqueous solution.
4. Gas from marble + dilute sulphuric acid — (d) carbon dioxide 1
Marble is calcium carbonate. Acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide. Hydrogen is the trap here: hydrogen comes from acid + metal, not from acid + carbonate.
Source: Ch 10, section 10.3 point 4, book p145 (CaCO3 + H2SO4). SLO: describe the characteristic properties of acids in terms of their reactions with metals, bases and carbonates.
5. Gas from ammonium chloride + sodium hydroxide — (a) ammonia 1
Bases decompose ammonium salts on heating: base + ammonium salt → salt + ammonia + water. NaOH + NH4Cl → NaCl + NH3 + H2O.
Source: Ch 10, section 10.4 point 5, book p146. SLO: identify the characteristic properties of bases in terms of their reactions with acids and ammonium salts.
6. Every acid solution contains an excess of — (b) H+ ions 1
An acid ionizes in water to give H+ ions; an alkali ionizes to give OH− ions. Getting this pair the right way round is the whole basis of the chapter.
Source: Ch 10, section 10.1.1, book p141. SLO: recognize that aqueous solutions of acids contain H+ ions and aqueous solutions of alkalis contain OH− ions.
7. Ordinary rain is slightly acidic because of dissolved — (c) carbon dioxide 1
The book is precise about this: normal rain is slightly acidic because of dissolved carbon dioxide. SO2 and NO2 are the acid oxides that come from human activity and turn normal rain into acid rain, which is a different thing.
Source: Ch 10, end of section 10.3, book p145. SLO: define acid rain.
8. [REVISION, Ch 8] Catalyst for hydrogenating vegetable oils into margarine — (d) nickel 1
Nickel is the catalyst for the manufacture of margarine. All four options are on the same list in her book, so learn them as one set: iron → Haber Process (synthesis of ammonia); platinum and palladium → catalytic converters in automobiles; nickel → manufacture of margarine.
Source: Ch 4, section 4.5 “Transition Elements”, point 5 “Catalysts for Industrial Processes”, book p64. Re-test: answered “platinum” on exam-22 Q6, and “nickel” for the Haber process on exam-21 Q14. This is the second re-test of this list.
9. [REVISION, Ch 4] Element X with configuration 2, 8, 2 — (a) Group 2, Period 3 1
Period = number of occupied shells = 3. Group for an s-block element = number of valence electrons = 2. The +10 correction belongs only to p-block elements, which is exactly the slip made on exam-22, where 2 + 10 was written for an s-block element.
Re-test: exam-22 Section B, element X (3s2) answered as Group 8 after adding then striking out 10. Ch 4 SLO: electronic configuration and position in the periodic table.
10. [REVISION, Ch 9] At dynamic equilibrium the forward rate is — (b) equal to the rate of the reverse reaction 1
At equilibrium the two rates are equal, not zero and not greater. Both reactions are still happening, which is why it is called dynamic; the amounts stop changing because the two rates cancel out.
Re-test: this exact pair (rates equal vs greater) was missed on the OPF First Term school paper, August 2026. Ch 9 SLO: dynamic equilibrium.

Marking rule used on this paper: a missing name or a missing unit loses ½ mark, the same in every question. It never cancels a correct equation or correct working.
2. (i) Define a Bronsted-Lowry acid and a Bronsted-Lowry base. 2
A Bronsted-Lowry acid is a proton donor (it gives away an H+ ion). 1
A Bronsted-Lowry base is a proton acceptor (it takes an H+ ion). 1
(ii) HCl + H2O → H3O+ + Cl− — identify the acid and the base, with a reason. 2
HCl is the Bronsted-Lowry acid, because it donates a proton and is converted into Cl−. 1
H2O is the Bronsted-Lowry base, because it accepts that proton and becomes the hydronium ion H3O+. 1
Useful check from the book: after donating a proton an acid forms a negative ion, and after accepting one a base forms a positive ion. Source: Ch 10, section 10.1.2 and Example 10.1, book p142–143.

5. (i) All alkalis are bases, but all bases are not alkalis. Explain. 2
An alkali is a base that is soluble in water. 1 Every alkali is therefore a base, but many bases do not dissolve in water, and those insoluble bases are not alkalis. So alkalis are a smaller group inside the bases. 1
(ii) One base that is an alkali, one that is not, with formulae. 2
Alkali: sodium hydroxide, NaOH (or KOH, or Ca(OH)2) 1
Not an alkali: copper hydroxide, Cu(OH)2 (or Al(OH)3, or Fe(OH)3) 1
Source: Ch 10, “Alkalis” paragraph, book p145.

9. [REVISION, Ch 8] Energy changes in a reaction. 8
(i) Define activation energy. 2
Her book's wording (section 8.5): activation energy is the minimum amount of energy that, in addition to the average kinetic energy, particles must have 1 in an effective collision. 1 Accept any answer carrying both ideas: a minimum energy, and that it is needed for an effective (successful) collision. The book adds that no reaction occurs if the energy of the reacting particles is lower than the activation energy.
(ii) Labelled energy-profile diagram for an exothermic reaction. 4
Solid black curve = the reaction without a catalyst. Red dashed curve = the same reaction with a catalyst.
The products sit lower than the reactants, which is what makes the reaction exothermic, so ΔH is negative.
Marks for the diagram: energy on the vertical axis and reaction pathway on the horizontal axis 1; products drawn below the reactants with both labelled 1; the hump drawn and Ea labelled from the reactants up to the top of the hump 1; ΔH labelled between the reactant and product levels, marked as negative 1.
(iii) The catalysed pathway. 2
The catalysed curve is drawn with a lower hump starting and ending at the same two levels. 1
Ea is lowered by the catalyst, but ΔH is completely unchanged, because the reactants and the products are still at the same energies as before. 1
The model for this diagram is Fig. 8.1 in her own book, “Energy level diagram for exothermic reaction” (p130), which carries exactly these labels: transition state, activation energy, reactants, enthalpy change ΔH < 0, products, reaction progress. The catalysed curve is Fig. 8.3 (p131). Marking note: on exam-22 this diagram was drawn with no Ea label and no catalysed curve, so both are explicitly demanded in the wording this time. The “catalyst does not change ΔH” point was fixed on exam-22 and is being held here. Ch 8 SLOs: activation energy, enthalpy change, and the effect of a catalyst.
