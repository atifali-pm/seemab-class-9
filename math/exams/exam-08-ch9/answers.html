SECTION A — MCQ Answer Key (12 × 1 = 12 Marks)
Q1. C — Axiom
An axiom is assumed true without proof (e.g., "a number equals itself"). A theorem requires proof; a conjecture needs verification.
Q2. B — Conjecture
A conjecture is believed true but unproven. Axioms are accepted without proof; theorems are already proved.
Q3. B — ∼
The similarity symbol is ∼. The congruence symbol ≅ means same shape and size. Similarity means same shape only.
Q4. B — k2
Ratio of areas = (ratio of corresponding lengths)2 = k2. Common error: choosing k (linear ratio) or k3 (volume ratio).
Q5. C — (n−2) × 180°
Formula comes from splitting an n-gon into (n−2) triangles. For n=4: (4−2)×180 = 360° (correct for quadrilateral).
Q6. C — 360°
Sum of exterior angles of any convex polygon is always 360°, regardless of the number of sides.
Q7. B — 120°
Regular hexagon: n=6. Interior angle = (6−2)×180/6 = 720/6 = 120°. Option A (108°) belongs to a regular pentagon.
Q8. B — ½ P × a
Area of regular polygon = ½ × Perimeter × apothem. Apothem is the perpendicular from centre to any side.
Q9. A — 3 : 2
V1/V2 = k3, so k3 = 27/8, thus k = 3/2. Ratio of lengths = 3 : 2. Option B (9:4) is the area ratio, not length.
Q10. C — Perpendicular bisector of AB
The perpendicular bisector of segment AB is the locus of all points equidistant from A and B. Every point on it satisfies PA = PB.
Q11. B — 8
n = 360° / exterior angle = 360/45 = 8 sides (regular octagon). Common error: dividing 180 by 45 giving 4.
Q12. B — n(n−3)/2
Each vertex connects to (n−3) non-adjacent vertices. Total unique diagonals = n(n−3)/2. Option A counts all vertex pairs including sides.
SECTION B — Short Questions (11 × 3 = 33 Marks)
2(i) — Axiom vs Conjecture 3 marks

Axiom: A mathematical statement assumed to be true without any proof. 1 mark
Example: "If a = b and b = c, then a = c" (Transitive property). Axioms are also called postulates when related to geometry.

Conjecture: A statement believed to be true but whose truth has not yet been proved. 1 mark
Example: From the pattern 4, 8, 12, 16, … one conjectures the next number is 20 (adding 4). This is plausible but not yet formally proved.

Key difference: An axiom is accepted without proof; a conjecture still requires verification or proof. 1 mark

OR
Theorem and Its Six Steps 3 marks

Theorem: A mathematical statement that can be proved or supposed provable through logical reasoning. 1 mark

Six steps of geometrical proof 2 marks (any 6 correctly named):

  1. Statement — describe the theorem in words.
  2. Figure — draw a neat labelled diagram.
  3. Given — write given information symbolically.
  4. Required to Prove — state the result to be proved symbolically.
  5. Construction — add any extra lines/points needed.
  6. Proof — write statements with reasons step by step.

2(vii) — Regular and Irregular Polygons 3 marks

Regular polygon: A polygon in which all sides and all interior angles are equal. Also called equilateral and equiangular. Examples: equilateral triangle, square. 1 mark

Irregular polygon: A polygon in which at least one side or one angle is not equal to the others. Examples: scalene triangle, rectangle (equal angles but unequal sides). 1 mark

Each interior angle of a regular n-gon = (n − 2) × 180° / n. 1 mark

3(i) — Reasoning Types and Algebraic Proof 5 marks

(a) Inductive Reasoning: Working from specific observations to a general result. It is not always logically valid. 1 mark
Example: Every day school starts at 8:00 am and you leave at 7:15 am and arrive on time. You conclude leaving at 7:15 always gets you there on time.

Deductive Reasoning: Working from a general rule to a specific conclusion. It is logically reliable. 1 mark
Example: All numbers equal themselves (axiom). Therefore 5 = 5.

(b) Inductive verification for (x+1)2 + 5 = x2 + 2x + 6: 1 mark

x = 2: LHS = (3)2 + 5 = 14; RHS = 4 + 4 + 6 = 14 ✓
x = 5: LHS = (6)2 + 5 = 41; RHS = 25 + 10 + 6 = 41 ✓
x = 10: LHS = (11)2 + 5 = 126; RHS = 100 + 20 + 6 = 126 ✓

Deductive proof (general): 2 marks
LHS = (x+1)2 + 5 = x2 + 2x + 1 + 5 = x2 + 2x + 6 = RHS. Hence proved for all real x.

OR
Definitions: Statement, Proposition, Corollary, Converse 5 marks

(a) Mathematical statement: A meaningful composition of words that can be either true or false; may contain words and symbols. 1 mark
Example: "Sum of 2 and 5 is 7" (true statement).

Proposition and its three parts 1 mark: (i) Premise (assumption), (ii) Argument (logical chain from premise to conclusion), (iii) Conclusion (result after applying the argument). The conclusion must be true when premises are true and argument is valid.

Corollary: A result that can be deduced directly from an already proved theorem, without much additional work. 1 mark

Converse of a theorem: Formed by interchanging the "given" and "to prove" parts. The converse is not necessarily a theorem. 1 mark

(b) Mathematical statements: 1 mark
(i) 34 + 16 ≠ 50: True mathematical statement (it is false as 34+16=50, so the ≠ makes it false, but it is still a statement).
(ii) (a+b)2 = a2 + 2ab + b2: True mathematical statement.
(iii) xy + z = 12: Open sentence (not a mathematical statement since values of x, y, z are unknown and truth value cannot be determined).

3(ii) — DE ∥ BC, Finding Ratios and Areas 5 marks

(a) In ΔABC, DE ∥ BC with D on AB and E on AC. By the Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales' theorem), a line parallel to one side of a triangle divides the other two sides proportionally. 2 marks
Therefore: AD/DB = AE/EC. Here AD = 4 cm, DB = 6 cm, so AD/DB = 4/6 = 2/3. ✓

(b) AE/EC = 2/3. Since AC = AE + EC = 15 cm: 1 mark
AE/(15 − AE) = 2/3 ⇒ 3 AE = 30 − 2 AE ⇒ 5 AE = 30 ⇒ AE = 6 cm.

(c) AD/AB = 4/(4+6) = 4/10 = 2/5. 2 marks
Area of ΔADE / Area of ΔABC = (AD/AB)2 = (2/5)2 = 4/25.
(If Area of ΔABC = 256 cm2, then Area ΔADE = (4/25) × 256 ≈ 40.96 cm2.)

Let Wajid's height = h and his shadow = (2/3)h. Sun angle is the same for Wajid and the pole, so the two triangles are similar (AA similarity). 1 mark

(a) Wajid's shadow / Wajid's height = Pole's shadow / Pole's height 2 marks
(2/3) = 12 / Pole height ⇒ Pole height = 12 / (2/3) = 12 × 3/2 = 18 m.

(c) Yes, the triangles are similar by AA similarity: both share the same sun angle (angle of elevation) and both have a right angle at ground level. 1 mark

Loci: Fixed Point, Isosceles Triangles, Three Non-Collinear Points 5 marks

(a) The locus of a point P equidistant from a fixed point O is a circle with centre O and radius equal to the fixed distance. 1 mark
Sketch: a circle centred at O with any radius r, where every point on the circumference is at distance r from O.

P / \ / r \ O------- (circle, centre O)

(b) ΔXAB and ΔYAB are isosceles with XA = XB and YA = YB (equal arms). 2 marks
Since XA = XB, X is equidistant from A and B ⇒ X lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB.
Since YA = YB, Y is also equidistant from A and B ⇒ Y also lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB.
Therefore X, Y, and their line XY all lie on the perpendicular bisector of AB, meaning XY bisects AB at right angles (perpendicularly). ✓

(c) Draw perpendicular bisectors of AB and BC. They intersect at a point O where OA = OB = OC. The locus is the single point O, called the circumcentre. 2 marks A circle drawn with O as centre and OA as radius passes through all three points and is called the circumcircle.
