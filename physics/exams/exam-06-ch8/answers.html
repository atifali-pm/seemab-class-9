SECTION A — Multiple Choice Questions (12 × 1 = 12 Marks)
Q.1: C — Electrons 1
Explanation: According to domain theory, magnetism arises from the motion of charged particles. Electrons orbit the nucleus and also spin, each producing a tiny magnetic field. Protons and neutrons are in the nucleus; their magnetic contribution is negligibly small compared to electrons.
Q.2: C — 1012 1
Explanation: The textbook states that a magnetic domain is a group of roughly 1012 atoms of size a few millimetres. Students commonly confuse 106 (a million) with 1012 (a trillion); the chapter specifies 1012 explicitly.
Q.3: C — North pole 1
Explanation: By convention, magnetic field lines originate from the N-pole and terminate at the S-pole outside the magnet, and travel from S to N inside it. Students often confuse the convention with the physical source of the field.
Q.4: B — Tesla 1
Explanation: The tesla (T) is the SI unit of magnetic field strength B, equal to N A−1 m−1. Weber is the unit of magnetic flux; Ampere is the unit of current; Henry is the unit of inductance.
Q.5: B — Induced magnet 1
Explanation: The textbook defines an induced magnet as “a material that becomes a magnet when placed in a magnetic field.” A permanent magnet retains magnetism without a field; diamagnetic materials are actually weakly repelled by a magnetic field.
Q.6: D — Solenoid method 1
Explanation: The chapter explicitly states that the solenoid method is “the most common method used for the magnetization of metals.” Domains align with the external field produced by the current-carrying coil wrapped around the metal.
Q.7: C — Soft iron 1
Explanation: Soft magnetic materials (like soft iron) easily magnetize and demagnetize, making them ideal for electromagnets where the field must be switched on and off. Hard magnetic materials (like steel) resist demagnetization and are used for permanent magnets.
Q.8: C — Zero 1
Explanation: In diamagnetic materials the spin and orbital motions of electrons cancel each other, giving a net magnetic field of zero per atom (B = 0). Paramagnetic materials have a small non-zero net field; ferromagnetic materials have a large net field.
Q.9: B — 11.3° 1
Explanation: The textbook states that Earth’s magnetic poles are inclined at 11.3° to the geographical poles. This is why a compass needle does not point exactly toward geographic north but deviates slightly toward the magnetic pole.
Q.10: D — Nickel 1
Explanation: Ferromagnetic materials include iron, cobalt, nickel, and some metallic alloys. Silver and copper are diamagnetic; aluminium is paramagnetic. Nickel is specifically listed in the chapter as a ferromagnetic material.
Q.11: C — Two complete magnets 1
Explanation: Magnets are always bipolar (dipole). Cutting a bar magnet creates two smaller magnets each with its own N and S pole. A monopole (a magnet with only one pole) does not exist. Students often think cutting makes them unmagnetized or produces monopoles.
Q.12: D — Motion of ions in the core 1
Explanation: Earth’s core mainly consists of molten iron. Electrical currents flowing in this slowly moving molten iron generate Earth’s magnetic field; this is called the dynamo effect. Rotation and spinning of Earth are related to the geographical axis, not the magnetic field source.
SECTION B — Short Answer Questions (11 × 3 = 33 Marks)
2(i) Domain Theory 3

Domain theory of magnetism: Magnetism arises because electrons (charged particles) move around the nucleus, producing tiny magnetic fields. In a sizeable piece of material, a group of roughly 1012 atoms whose N-poles point in the same direction forms a magnetic domain (size: a few mm). 1

In an unmagnetized material domains are randomly oriented so their fields cancel and the net field is zero. 1

In a magnetized material (placed in an external magnetic field) all domains align in the direction of the applied field, giving the material a net non-zero magnetic field. 1

OR

Electrons and atomic magnetism 3

Every electron has two motions: (a) orbital motion around the nucleus, producing an orbital magnetic moment, and (b) spin motion, producing a spin magnetic moment. Each electron therefore acts as a tiny magnet. 1

In some atoms these electron magnetic moments add up to a non-zero net field, making the material magnetic; in others they cancel. 1

The spinning nucleus also produces a tiny magnetism, but it is negligibly small compared to electron contributions and is therefore ignored in the study of magnetism. 1

2(ii) Magnetic Field 3

Magnetic field: “The region or space around a magnet where it exerts a force on other magnetic poles is called the magnetic field.” 1

SI unit of B: Tesla (T), which equals N A−1 m−1. 1

Direction of B at a point: determined by placing a north-pole at that point. The force experienced by the north-pole gives the direction of the magnetic field at that point. On a curved field line, the direction is found by drawing a tangent at that point. 1

OR

Magnetic field lines 3

Magnetic field lines are curves that represent the magnetic field graphically. Three properties: 1 per property

  • They originate from the N-pole and terminate at the S-pole outside the magnet (and travel S to N inside).
  • They never intersect each other (because the field can have only one direction at any given point).
  • Where field lines are closer together the field is stronger; where they are farther apart the field is weaker.
2(iii) Relative Strength of Magnetic Field 3

Magnetic field lines give both direction and strength of the field at any point. 1

The field is stronger where lines are closer together and weaker where lines are farther apart. This spacing is called the relative strength (degree of closeness) of the field lines. 1

By placing two like poles close together a neutral zone (field-free region) forms between them where the field lines repel each other and cancel. Placing unlike poles close together strengthens the field between them. 1

OR

Magnetic Shielding 3

Magnetic shielding: the phenomenon in which some region of space is made free from a magnetic field by suitably arranging magnets to create a neutral zone. 1

The neutral zone is created by placing two N-poles side by side so their field lines repel each other, making a field-free region between them. Devices like power inverters and magnetic sensors must be shielded from external fields. 1

Three shield materials: iron, nickel, and cobalt. Shields are made with rounded corners because magnetic field lines cannot easily turn at a 90° angle, so sharp corners would let field lines pass through. 1

2(iv) Induced Magnetism & Stroking Method 3

Induced magnetism: the phenomenon in which a non-magnetic material is made into a magnet using various techniques. A material that becomes a magnet when placed in a field is called an induced magnet. 1

Stroking method: a permanent magnet is stroked from one end of a steel bar to the other, always in the same direction. The poles of the bar material align with the stroking magnet. 1

After one stroke the magnet is lifted and returned to the starting end before the next stroke. The end where stroking finishes becomes a south pole (opposite to the pole of the magnet that last left that end). 1

OR

Hammering Method 3

In the hammering method a bar of metal is placed inside a strong external magnetic field and then hammered gently. The vibration caused by hammering allows domains to align in the direction of the applied field. 1

This method is mainly used for the magnetization of steel. 1

The magnetization can be increased by heating the metal bar slightly before hammering, which makes domain alignment easier. 1

2(v) Heating Method (Demagnetization) 3

The heating method is usually used to demagnetize a material. Heat speeds up the random movement of already-aligned domains, causing misalignment of domains, so the material loses its magnetization. 1

Demagnetization by heat is irreversible once the material cools randomly. 1

The magnetic Seebeck effect (thermo-magnetism): recent evidence shows that heating can also generate a magnetic field in certain materials. This is the reverse of the usual demagnetization effect and is still an active research topic. 1

OR

Solenoid Method & AC vs DC 3

A solenoid is a coil of wire wrapped around a cylindrical core. When a metal is placed inside and current flows through the coil, the magnetic field of the solenoid acts as an external field and aligns the domains of the metal, magnetizing it. 1

Direct current (DC) keeps the polarity of the solenoid constant, so domains align in one fixed direction; the material is magnetized. 1

Alternating current (AC) reverses the polarity every half cycle: in the first half cycle the material magnetizes in one direction, then demagnetizes; in the second half it magnetizes in the opposite direction, then demagnetizes again. The net result is demagnetization. 1

2(vi) B = μonI (Solenoid Formula) 3

The magnetic field strength inside a solenoid is given by: B = μonI 1

  • B: magnetic field strength, unit = tesla (T)
  • μo: permeability of free space (vacuum) = 4π × 10−7 N A−2
  • n: number of turns per unit length (m−1), n = N/L
  • I: current through the solenoid, unit = ampere (A)

1 for correct definitions and units.

Permeability is the property of a material measuring how much it permits a magnetic field to pass through it. 1

Soft iron vs Steel 3

Soft iron is a soft magnetic material: it easily magnetizes and just as easily demagnetizes when the current is removed. This is essential for electromagnets, which must turn on and off repeatedly. 1

Steel is a hard magnetic material: once magnetized it retains its magnetism even when the external field is removed. This property makes it ideal for permanent magnets. 1

In general: soft magnetic materials (low retentivity) are used for electromagnets/temporary purposes; hard magnetic materials (high retentivity) are used for permanent magnets. 1

Ferromagnetic Materials 3

Ferromagnetic materials: materials that have a net magnetism at the atomic level even without an external field. 1

Three distinguishing properties: (a) strongly attracted to a magnet (unlike paramagnetic which are only feebly attracted), (b) they retain magnetism after the external field is removed, and (c) their magnetic field intensity is very large (unlike the small field of paramagnetic materials). 1

Examples: iron, cobalt, nickel, and some metallic alloys. 1

Can field lines intersect? 3

No, magnetic field lines can never intersect each other. 1

The direction of the magnetic field at any point is determined by the direction of the force on a north-pole placed at that point. 1

If two field lines intersected at a point, then the field at that point would have two directions simultaneously, which is physically impossible. Therefore field lines never cross. 1

2(x) Earth’s Magnetic Field and the Dynamo Effect 3

Earth is a large magnet with its own magnetic field called the geomagnetic field. It extends millions of kilometres into space but has a very small field strength. 1

Earth’s core mainly consists of molten iron. Electrical currents flowing in this slowly moving molten iron generate the magnetic field of Earth; this is called the dynamo effect. 1

Earth’s field acts as a protective shield, saving life from harmful cosmic rays and charged particles and radiation from the Sun. The field resembles that of a bar magnet. 1

OR

Geographical vs Magnetic Poles 3

Earth spins about its geographical axis, with the geographical north pole at the Arctic and the south pole at the Antarctic. 1

The magnetic poles of Earth are inclined at an angle of 11.3° to the geographical poles. They are therefore not at the same location. 1

Practical effect: a compass needle does not point exactly toward geographic north; instead it points 11.3° away toward the magnetic pole. Navigators must account for this magnetic declination. 1

2(xi) Speakers 3

A speaker uses two magnets: a permanent magnet of strong magnetization fixed at the centre of the cone (diaphragm) and an electromagnet (voice coil) attached to the centre of the permanent magnet. 1

When a sound signal (as alternating electric current) flows through the electromagnet coil, it produces an alternating magnetic field which interacts with the permanent magnet’s field, causing the electromagnet to move to and fro. Since the cone is attached to the electromagnet, it vibrates and produces sound. 1

The commonly used permanent magnet material in speakers is neodymium. A larger permanent magnet produces louder sound. 1

OR

Magnetic Recording 3

Magnetic recording is the method of saving sounds, pictures, and data in the form of electrical signals by selective magnetization of portions of a magnetic material. 1

A tape head moves across the tape. The signal current flowing through the tape head creates a magnetic field that aligns domains in the tape’s magnetic emulsion, recording the data. Reading is the reverse process. 1

Three recording media: iron oxide, cobalt, chromium oxide (and pure iron). Two recording devices: magnetic tape recorders and disk recorders. 1

SECTION C — Long Questions (4 × 5 = 20 Marks)
3(i) Domain Theory of Magnetism 5

(a) How electrons produce magnetism at the atomic level 1

Everything is made of atoms with a nucleus (positive charge) and orbiting electrons (negative charge). In late 20th century science established that the basis of magnetism is the motion of charged particles. Each electron has two motions: (i) orbital motion around the nucleus producing an orbital magnetic moment, and (ii) spin producing a spin magnetic moment. In some atoms these moments add up to a non-zero net field, making the material magnetic.

(b) Definition and typical size of a domain 1

A magnetic domain is a group of atoms in a material whose N-poles all point in the same direction. A typical domain contains roughly 1012 atoms and has a size of a few millimetres.

(c) Domain arrangement before and after magnetization 2

  • Before (unmagnetized): domains are randomly oriented; their fields cancel and the net magnetic field is zero.
  • After (magnetized): domains align in the direction of the applied external magnetic field; the material has a net non-zero magnetic field and acts as a magnet.

(d) Effect of heavy heating 1

Heavy heating speeds up random movement of already-aligned domains, causing them to misalign. The material loses its magnetization. This is why the heating method is used for demagnetization.

OR

Magnetic Field and Field Lines 5

(a) Plotting field lines 1

Using a compass: place the bar magnet on a sheet; note where compass needles point around it; the pattern of arrows traces the field lines. Using iron filings: scatter iron filings around the magnet; they align along field lines showing the same pattern as compass needles.

(b) Direction of field lines 1

Outside the magnet: lines emerge from the N-pole and enter the S-pole. Inside the magnet body: lines travel from S-pole to N-pole, forming closed loops.

(c) Spacing and field strength 1

The closer the field lines, the stronger the magnetic field at that point. Conversely, widely spaced lines indicate a weaker field. The relative strength is the degree of closeness of the field lines.

(d) Neutral zone 2

A neutral zone (field-free region) is formed by placing two N-poles side by side. Their field lines seem to repel each other, creating a region between them where the net field is zero. This phenomenon is called magnetic shielding of a region and is exploited to protect sensitive electronics from stray fields.

3(iv) Earth’s Magnetic Field 5

(a) Dynamo effect 1

Earth’s core mainly consists of molten iron. Electrical currents flowing in the slowly moving molten iron generate Earth’s magnetic field; this process is called the dynamo effect. Earth is therefore effectively a large magnet with its own magnetic field, also known as the geomagnetic field.

(b) Resemblance to a bar magnet 1

Earth’s magnetic field resembles the field of a bar magnet: it has a N-pole and S-pole, and field lines emerge at one pole, loop around, and enter at the other. On the Sun-facing side the field is compressed by solar winds; on the opposite side it extends several million kilometres into space.

(c) Angle between geographical and magnetic poles 1

Earth’s magnetic poles are inclined at 11.3° to the geographical poles. The magnetic axis and geographical axis are therefore not the same, which is why a compass needle does not point exactly toward geographic north but instead deviates 11.3° toward the magnetic pole.

(d) Protection from cosmic rays and solar radiation 1

Earth’s magnetic field acts as a protective shield around Earth. It deflects harmful cosmic rays coming from outer space and the charged particles and radiation (solar wind) streaming from the Sun. Without this shield, life on Earth could not survive the constant bombardment of high-energy particles.

(e) Aurora 1

The Aurora (dancing lights in the sky) forms at the northern and southern poles of Earth. Earth’s magnetic field has polar cusps where charged particles from the Sun enter the atmosphere. When these particles collide with atoms of air (gases), they ionize them, which causes emission of light of different colours depending on the nature of the gas, producing the Aurora.

Magnetic Recording + Speakers and Microphones 5

(a) Magnetic Recording 2

Magnetic recording is the method of saving sounds, pictures, and data as electrical signals by selective magnetization of portions of a magnetic material.

WRITING (Recording): Signal current --> Tape head (electromagnet) --> Tape head moves over magnetic tape --> Magnetic field of tape head aligns domains in tape emulsion --> Pattern of domains = encoded data READING (Playback): Tape moves under tape head --> Aligned domains in tape induce a current in tape head --> Current decoded as audio/video/data signal

Three recording media: iron oxide, cobalt, chromium oxide. Two recording devices: magnetic tape recorders (audio/video) and disk recorders (computer storage). Also: magnetic drums, core and bubble units. 1

(b) Speakers and Microphones 2

Speaker: A permanent magnet is fixed at the centre of the cone (diaphragm). An electromagnet (voice coil) is attached at the same centre. When an audio signal (AC) flows through the voice coil, its alternating field interacts with the permanent magnet’s field, pushing and pulling the voice coil back and forth. The attached cone vibrates, moving air and producing sound. Permanent magnet material: neodymium.

Microphone: works in reverse. Sound waves cause the cone (diaphragm) to vibrate, which moves the electromagnet within the field of the permanent magnet. This movement generates an alternating electric current in the coil, which becomes the audio signal. The microphone therefore converts mechanical energy (vibration) into electrical energy, while the speaker converts electrical energy into mechanical energy (sound). 1
