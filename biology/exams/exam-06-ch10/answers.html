SECTION A — Multiple Choice Questions (12 × 1 = 12 Marks)
Q1. Evolution is defined as the gradual change in a population's traits in response to: B — Environmental conditions 1 mark
Evolution is the gradual alteration of a population's or species' traits over time in response to the environmental conditions. Evolutionary changes can always be passed on to next generations. Food supply, geographic barriers, and reproductive rate are contributing factors but are not the definition.
Q2. Which is NOT a listed source of variation: C — Photosynthesis 1 mark
The textbook lists five sources: genetic recombination (crossing over), independent assortment of chromosomes, fertilization, mutation in gamete-forming cells, and gene flow. Photosynthesis is a metabolic process and is not a source of variation.
Q3. Darwin's voyage was on the ship: B — HMS Beagle 1 mark
After a five-year voyage around the world on the HMS Beagle, Charles Darwin put forth the Theory of Natural Selection in 1838. In 1859 he published On the Origin of Species by Natural Selection. Victory, Endeavour, and Discovery were other famous ships unrelated to Darwin.
Q4. An inherited trait that increases survival chances is called: C — Adaptation 1 mark
An adaptation is an inherited trait (or group of traits) that increases an organism's chances of survival in specific environmental conditions. Variation refers to differences in traits; mutation is a gene change; speciation is the formation of new species.
Q5. What most accurately measures an organism's fitness: B — How many fertile offspring it produces 1 mark
In the context of natural selection, fitness is measured by reproductive success: an organism that produces more fertile offspring passes more of its genes to the next generation. Withstanding extremes or eating well are useful but secondary to actual reproduction.
Q6. Peppered moths before 1848 were mostly: B — Light colour with dark patches 1 mark
All reported variants of the peppered moth up until 1848 had light colours with dark patches. A dark-coloured moth was first discovered in 1848. By 1895 dark-coloured moths made up 95% of the population due to Industrial Melanism.
Q7. Limb bones of humans, dogs, birds, whales with same structure are: C — Homologous structures 1 mark
Homologous structures share the same fundamental shape due to origin from a common ancestor, though their shapes and sizes have evolved for different functions. Vestigial structures are inactive remnants; analogous structures have similar function but different origin (not in this textbook).
Q8. A mutation is: C — A change in gene 1 mark
A mutation is a change in the gene. Mutations in gamete-forming germ cells can be inherited and serve as a source of variation. A change in appearance may be the result of a mutation but is not the definition; a change in behaviour is not a mutation.
Q9. Neli-Ravi buffalo were produced by: C — Selective breeding 1 mark
Selective breeding (artificial selection) is the practice of breeding domesticated animals to produce offspring with desired genetic features. Cows/buffalo producing more milk are chosen as parents for the next generation, creating breeds that outperform the original.
Q10. Speciation is the evolutionary process by which: B — A new species is formed 1 mark
Speciation is defined as the evolutionary process by which new biological species arise. A breed is produced by artificial selection; a race and a population are not new biological species by definition.
Q11. Vestigial organs are: B — Inactive; remains from a previous common ancestor 1 mark
Vestigial organs are inactive, non-functional structures that are likely just the remains from a previous common ancestor. Examples include wings on flightless birds, leaves on some cacti, and the appendix in humans. They are not newly evolved, not found only in plants, and are distinct from homologous structures.
Q12. Natural selection operates to produce changes in: B — Populations 1 mark
Natural selection causes population-wide changes in gene frequencies over time. It does not act on individuals (individuals don't evolve; they live or die). It operates at the level of the whole population, shifting how common or rare certain traits are over generations.

SECTION C — Long Questions (4 × 5 = 20 Marks)
3(i) Darwin's Theory of Natural Selection in detail (four bases). 5 marks

Natural selection is the process through which organisms who possess traits that are helpful for reproduction in a certain environment produce more children in the following generation, gradually expanding the population's genetic diversity. 1 mark

The theory is based on four interconnected points:

  1. Overproduction: All living creatures are very fertile so their populations could grow quickly to produce large numbers of offspring. This means not all can survive. 1 mark
  2. Competition: Population sizes do not rise to very high levels even after overproduction because food supplies and other resources are scarce. This creates a struggle for existence. 1 mark
  3. Variations: No two organisms are the same; a large portion of this diversity is heritable, inherited through the genes of offspring. Those with favourable variations have a survival advantage. 1 mark
  4. Natural selection: Only those organisms with desirable variations will live until reproductive age. The population will have more advantageous genetic variations and fewer harmful ones after many generations. Natural selection is essentially an elimination process; only the fittest organisms survive. 1 mark
OR
Darwin's observations on the HMS Beagle voyage and the Galapagos finches. 5 marks

After a five-year voyage around the world on the ship HMS Beagle, Charles Darwin put forth the Theory of Natural Selection in 1838. 1 mark

Darwin was able to create his theory when he visited the Galapagos Islands. He found a number of finch species that varied from island to island. 1 mark

He noticed that the form of the birds' beaks varied depending on the kind of food they ate (woodpecker finch used tools, seed-eaters had thick beaks, insect-eaters had slender beaks, etc.). 1 mark

Darwin believed that speciation and adaptation were linked: the overall accumulation of adaptations in different environments, isolated from the original environment by geographic barriers, would lead to the formation of a new species from an ancestral form. 1 mark

Darwin identified three basic patterns in biological diversity: (1) global, (2) local, and (3) variation over time in species. The two groups could differ sufficiently over many generations to be classified as two different species. 1 mark

3(ii) Peppered moth case study: colour shift during and after the Industrial Revolution. 5 marks

Pre-industrial phase (before 1848): All peppered moths (Biston betularia) had light colours with dark patches. The moths rest on tree trunks throughout the day. Light-coloured lichens growing on the trunks blended with the colour of the light-coloured moth, giving excellent concealment from predator birds. Dark moths were rare (from a mutation). 1 mark

During the Industrial Revolution (1848 onward): Sulphur dioxide pollution from coal burning drove out lichens from trees in industrial locations, exposing the darker bark. Light-coloured moths resting on dark-coloured trunks were now easily visible. Predator birds could locate and consume the light-coloured moths easily. 1 mark

Selection mechanism: Dark colour was the adaptive variable: dark moths resting on dark trunks were hidden from the birds. Predator birds served as snoopers, picking off the visible light moths. By 1895, dark-coloured moths made up 95% of the population. This phenomenon is called Industrial Melanism. 2 marks

Post-1950s reversal: After England's industrial pollution was reduced, lichens returned to tree trunks, the bark lightened, and the light-coloured moths regained their camouflage advantage. The number of light-coloured moths increased again, confirming that natural selection reverses when the environment changes back. 1 mark

3(iv) Speciation: geographical isolation, reproductive isolation, and Darwin's three patterns. 5 marks

Speciation is the evolutionary process by which new biological species arise. Two groups that originate from the same ancestor can become very different over time if they live in different places. 1 mark

Geographical isolation: When a species gets split into two geographical regions, the process of speciation starts. Each group adapts to its own environmental conditions independently. 1 mark

Darwin's three patterns of biological diversity: (1) global variation (differences between species across the whole world), (2) local variation (differences between species in one area), and (3) variation over time (species change their form across geological time). 1 mark
